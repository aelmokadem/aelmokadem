Free Will: Did You Really Choose to Read This?

PHILOSOPHY
FREE WILL
DETERMINISM
HUMAN NATURE
We make choices every day, but do we really choose what we want? An exploration of the unsettling possibility that free will is an illusion.
Author

Ahmed Elmokadem

Published

26 November 2026

An Unnerving Idea!

So, you decided to read this article. Well, good for you! It’s a wonderful read, let me tell you. But did you really choose to read it?

Before you dismiss the question as a stupid philosophical exercise, think about how you ended up here.

Perhaps one of your parents loved philosophy and got you asking big questions as a kid. Perhaps a recent conversation got you thinking about how we make choices. Maybe you’re my son and felt obligated to read this to make your dad happy. Or maybe you just had too much time on your hands and nothing better to do.

Whatever brought you here, chances are it was a mix of what you already cared about and what happened to catch your attention, much of it beyond your control.

So, if our decisions are shaped by things we had no say in, what does that mean for free will? Do we really have it? Or are we merely passengers in a universe where our sense of choice is an elaborate illusion?

This reminds me of a famous thought experiment proposed by the American philosopher John Searle in 1980, known as the Chinese room [1].

1.
Searle JR (1980) Minds, brains, and programs. Behavioral and Brain Sciences 3(3):417–424. https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X00005756

Imagine a person sitting inside a room who doesn’t understand a single word of Chinese. Messages written in Chinese are passed into the room through a slot, and the person inside has an elaborate set of instructions that allows them to manipulate the Chinese symbols and produce appropriate responses without ever understanding their meaning.

To a native Chinese speaker outside the room, it might appear that whoever is inside understands Chinese perfectly. Yet, according to Searle’s argument, the ability to produce appropriate responses by following a set of rules doesn’t necessarily amount to genuine understanding.1

1 Searle’s argument remains controversial. One prominent objection, known as the systems reply, suggests that although the person inside the room may not understand Chinese, the system as a whole might. The thought experiment is used here as an analogy rather than as evidence against free will.

Now, Searle’s thought experiment was concerned with understanding rather than free will, but I find the parallel intriguing.

Could something similar be happening with our sense of agency? Could what we perceive as free choices emerge from an extraordinarily complex set of underlying processes?

Imagine a computer program that can learn from its experiences, evaluate different options, modify its behavior, and even explain why it made a particular decision. Its decisions could be incredibly complex, perhaps even impossible for us to predict.

But just because we can’t predict a decision doesn’t mean it isn’t determined. Even in a completely deterministic system, we might simply lack the information or computing power needed to predict its behavior. Determinism and predictability are not the same thing.

Yet, despite all that complexity, we might still hesitate to say that the program possesses free will.

But then, what makes us fundamentally different? Could our genetics, upbringing, memories, emotions, and experiences collectively constitute our own rather sophisticated decision-making machinery?

And unlike the person inside the Chinese room, we might not even be aware of most of the rules we’re following.

To explore that possibility, let’s start with something that seems central to almost every decision we make: what we want.

You Can Do What You Want, But Can You Want What You Want?

Before you start cussing at me, let me commit the philosophical sin of appealing to authority and call on a guy a little bit smarter than me. Enter Arthur Schopenhauer, the great German philosopher.

In his prize-winning essay On the Freedom of the Will, Schopenhauer argued that what we do in a given situation follows from our character and the motives at play, neither of which we ultimately chose [2].2

2.
Schopenhauer A (2009) Prize essay on the freedom of the will. In: Janaway C (ed) The two fundamental problems of ethics. Cambridge University Press, pp 31–112

2 Schopenhauer’s essay won a prize from the Royal Norwegian Society of Sciences and Letters in 1839. His broader philosophical position is consistent with the implication I’m exploring here, so my rather presumptuous challenge is more an extension of his argument than a disagreement with it.

3.
Einstein A (1932) My credo

In My Credo, Einstein put it this way, crediting Schopenhauer [3]:

Man can do what he wants, but he cannot will what he wills.

There’s a subtle but profound difference between doing what we want and deciding what we want in the first place.

Imagine that I ask you to stop loving someone you deeply love. Not to pretend that you don’t love them, but to genuinely stop loving them, right here, right now.

Or consider your sexual orientation. You can make choices about whether and how to act on your attractions, but did you ever consciously choose whom you would find sexually attractive? Could you simply decide to be attracted to someone you’re not?

Or perhaps something a little less personal. Think of a food you absolutely despise. Can you simply decide to find it delicious?

In all these cases, you may have some control over how you act on your feelings and desires. But can you summon or dismiss those feelings and desires at will?

Of course, some of our preferences can change over time. We can develop an appreciation for things we once disliked, abandon old habits, and even deliberately work toward changing aspects of our personalities.

But where does the desire to change those preferences come from?

Now, if I may be so bold as to push Schopenhauer’s argument a little further (and risk getting into trouble with Einstein while I’m at it), can we really claim to freely do what we want if we cannot freely will what we want?

If what we do ultimately stems from what we want, and we have no ultimate control over what we want, wouldn’t it follow that we have no ultimate control over what we do either?

We may be free to act on our wants without being the ultimate authors of our actions.

Let’s put this idea to the test.

A Mango or a Fig?

Imagine you are offered two pieces of fruit, a mango and a fig, and you choose the mango.

Why?

Well, it’s obvious. You like mangoes and hate figs (can’t blame you there), so you want the mango more.

But did you choose to like mangoes more than figs? Did you, at some point in your life, consciously decide what your taste buds should find appealing?

Probably not.

So, while nothing was stopping you from picking either fruit, you didn’t necessarily choose the preference that guided your decision.

I see you fussing again. You can choose what to do with your wants, right? You can pick the fig even if you’d rather eat the mango.

Think about the last time you said, “I really don’t want to work out today,” and went anyway. You probably meant it. You wanted to stay on the couch. You just wanted to keep the promise you’d made to yourself a little more.

Hate to burst your bubble here, my friend, but you may have picked the fig for a similar reason. You wanted to go against your first impulse.

Maybe you picked the fig out of curiosity. Maybe you thought it was time you gave figs another chance. Or maybe you just desperately wanted to prove me wrong.

I’d still ask where that curiosity or judgment came from. How much say did you have in becoming the person who would give the fig a second chance?

Maybe you worked at becoming more adventurous. But what made you want to do that? And did you choose that want too?

You can see where this is going.

Every time we try to explain how we chose one of our desires, we seem to appeal to another desire, value, or motivation that preceded it. We end up with what we might call a regress of desires.

And by desires or wants here, I don’t just mean cravings or immediate impulses. I’m also talking about our values, ambitions, convictions, and the reasons that motivate us to act, even when doing so is unpleasant.

We can keep tracing our choices back to the motivations behind them, and those motivations back to earlier ones. But can we ever trace them back to a starting point that we were entirely responsible for?

After all, we didn’t choose our genetic makeup, the circumstances into which we were born, or the experiences that shaped us before we were even capable of making conscious decisions.

And even when we began making conscious decisions, we did so as individuals who had already been shaped, to some extent, by those things.

This line of reasoning is closely related to what the philosopher Galen Strawson calls the Basic Argument. He argues that to be ultimately responsible for what we do, we would have to be ultimately responsible for the way we are. But any attempt to change who we are necessarily starts with the person we already are [4].3

4.
Strawson G (1994) The impossibility of moral responsibility. Philosophical Studies 75(1–2):5–24. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00989879

3 Strawson’s Basic Argument is primarily concerned with ultimate moral responsibility. Importantly, his argument is not dependent on determinism being true. Introducing randomness into the processes that shape our character would not, by itself, make us ultimately responsible for that character.

It’s not that we’re incapable of shaping who we become. We clearly can. The problem is that we never get to choose who we are before we begin doing the choosing.

Whatever control we exercise over ourselves seems to rest, ultimately, on foundations we did not choose.

But Aren’t These Wants Mine?

Now, you might reasonably object that I’m asking too much of free will.

Why should I have to be the ultimate creator of my desires for my choices to be considered free?

After all, my memories, experiences, preferences, values, and personality are a large part of what makes me me.

If my decision emerges from these things, why shouldn’t it be considered my own decision?

That’s one way to make the case for compatibilism, the view that we can have free will even if determinism is true.

Say you eat a mango because you enjoy mangoes. Now imagine eating the same mango because someone threatens you.

Even if both actions were entirely determined by earlier events, there would still be a meaningful difference between them. In the first case, you’re acting on your own preferences. In the second, you’re acting under coercion.

A compatibilist might say that’s what matters for free will. We can act freely when we’re guided by our own motives, values, and reasoning and have room to act on them. The fact that we didn’t ultimately choose those motives doesn’t necessarily make our actions any less free.

The philosopher Harry Frankfurt offers an interesting take on this by distinguishing between freedom of action and freedom of the will [5].

5.
Frankfurt HG (1971) Freedom of the will and the concept of a person. The Journal of Philosophy 68(1):5–20. https://doi.org/10.2307/2024717

Remember our mango and fig dilemma? You might want the mango but also wish that your curiosity about the fig would prevail. In other words, you have a desire, but you also have a desire about which of your desires you want to guide your actions.

On Frankfurt’s account, freedom of the will has to do with whether the desire you want to guide your actions is the one that actually does.4

4 Frankfurt distinguishes between first-order desires (desires to do something), second-order desires (desires about one’s desires), and second-order volitions (desires concerning which first-order desire should constitute one’s effective will). His account is one influential approach to understanding freedom of the will.

And I think that’s a perfectly reasonable position.

But you can probably guess what I’m going to ask next. Did you choose to want your curiosity to prevail over your appetite for the mango?

Aren’t we back to the same regress of desires we were wrestling with earlier, just one level up?

Even if I accept that my desires, values, and judgments are genuinely mine, I still wonder how much say I had in becoming the person who has them.

Of course, a compatibilist might argue that I don’t need to have that kind of control for my will to be considered free.

Perhaps the disagreement here is partly about what we mean by the word free.

Are we talking about the freedom to act and make decisions according to who we are, or the freedom to ultimately determine who we are in the first place?

The former seems considerably easier to defend than the latter.

A Rather Convincing Illusion?

I know the idea that we might not as free as we think we are is hard to digest, especially because our daily experiences keep reinforcing the notion that we have free will.

When we face a difficult decision, we can spend hours, days, or even years mulling it over. We weigh the pros and cons, imagine different futures, talk it through with other people, and sometimes change our minds several times before we settle on anything.

Surely, all that deliberation must count for something?

Absolutely!

Our ability to deliberate, reason, exercise self-control, and resist our impulses is real. These are all processes through which we arrive at decisions, and the decisions we make can be very different because of them.

But all that deliberation still draws on who we’ve become. What matters to us, which memories come to mind, how we evaluate the available options, and how readily we change our minds all shape how we think a decision through.

The fact that we’re actively involved in making a decision doesn’t necessarily mean that we’re the ultimate originators of the processes through which we make it.

Perhaps what we interpret as free will is simply what it feels like to make a decision from the inside.

The experience of making a choice is real. What might be an illusion is the belief that we are the ultimate originators of that choice.

And perhaps our sense of agency serves a useful purpose.

Imagine how difficult it might be to navigate life if we constantly perceived ourselves as nothing more than the products of countless preceding events. Instead, we experience ourselves as individuals capable of evaluating possibilities, pursuing goals, and influencing our surroundings.

Could this sense of agency be something that emerged through evolution because it helped us navigate the world more effectively?

I don’t know. But it’s an intriguing possibility.

Perhaps a cosmic trick, if you will, although one that the universe didn’t necessarily intend to play on us.

So, Why Bother?

At this point, you might be wondering why you should bother doing anything if you don’t ultimately have free will.

Why work hard? Why exercise? Why study? Why try to become a better person?

Well, because all of these things still have consequences!

There’s an important distinction between determinism and fatalism.

Under determinism, the state of the world at a given moment and the laws of nature fix what happens next. Our choices and actions are part of how that future comes about. The kind of fatalism I’m talking about says we’d end up in the same place whatever we did.

Imagine a student preparing for an exam. Maybe a lousy grade on the last test was what got them to open the textbook. The time they spend studying can still help them do better this time. That lousy grade, the decision to study, and the studying itself can all be part of the chain that leads to a better result.

The possibility that their motivation to study was determined by earlier events doesn’t make the studying any less consequential.

And the fact that we didn’t choose everything that shaped us doesn’t mean that we can’t play a role in shaping who we become.

In fact, it’s precisely because our experiences shape us that we can change.

Reading a book, having a conversation, making a mistake, or running into a new idea can change what we do next.

Even this article, believe it or not, might become one of the many factors influencing the way you think about things from now on.

So perhaps describing ourselves as mere passengers in the universe, as I did at the beginning of this article, isn’t entirely fair.

We are participants in the universe. We influence it just as it influences us.

Whether we do so with free will is an entirely different question.

What Does Science Have to Say?

Of course, everything we’ve discussed so far has been largely philosophical.

We’ve questioned whether we have ultimate control over our choices, but we haven’t established that the universe itself is deterministic. Those are two different questions.

Notice that the regress of desires we’ve been discussing doesn’t necessarily require a deterministic universe. Even if some of the processes that shape our desires involve an element of randomness, would that make those desires any more freely chosen?

So, what happens when we turn to science?

What does Einstein’s theory of relativity tell us about the nature of time and the future that we believe we are free to shape?

Could the past, present, and future all be equally real, with our entire lives embedded within a four-dimensional universe?

And what about the wonderfully bizarre world of quantum mechanics? Could the apparent randomness at the heart of quantum physics offer us an escape from determinism? And would such an escape necessarily grant us free will?

Well, that’s a rabbit hole for another article.

Have I brought you guys down yet? I hope not, but if I have, I apologize.

You might think about all this for a few seconds or even a few days. Then you’ll go on with your life, making choices as if you have free will.

Because let’s face it, you can’t help it. 🙂
